# More Than Salaam

### A love story for Shajer

For Shajer,
who turned one small word into the beginning of everything.

With all my love, Waqas

*Fifty days before forever · 11 November 2026*

## Before the First Page

This is a story about two people, one small word, and a great deal of silence that slowly learned how to talk.

Most of this story happened. The dates, messages, calls, promises and milestones are drawn from Waqas and Shajer’s real relationship. The telling has been shaped: scenes are arranged, transitions tightened, and descriptions sometimes give a memory its atmosphere. That shaping should never be mistaken for a new event. Messages reproduced from the source are kept as written. Where Waqas imagines what Shajer might have thought, it is only his guess, and she is welcome to correct it. The final part looks ahead to the wedding and the life after it. It is hopeful fiction, written with love rather than prophecy.

If this story has a soundtrack, it begins with a piano playing in another room. It carries the warmth of a Korean romance drama, the humour of two people learning each other, and the faith of two hearts that keep saying Alhamdulillah.

Read it with something warm to drink. And when you reach the umbrella scene, remember that the future pages are wishes, not memories.

## Prologue · Two Versions

Every love story has at least two versions, and the people inside it will never agree on either.

In Waqas’s version, it began with a decision. An invitation travelled between two homes. There were conversations between elders, visits, photographs, and a feeling that events were moving faster than he could explain. He did not know Shajer yet. He knew only what he had been told. And then, on the twentieth of August, an aunt held out a phone just before Maghrib, and he saw a photograph.

Light green clothes. A smile.

One word left him: Alhamdulillah.

In Shajer’s version, perhaps, the story began somewhere else: in a family conversation, in the decision to say yes to a visit, in the quiet curiosity of wondering who this person might be. Waqas will never pretend to know every thought she had. He has been learning them slowly, one message at a time, and he suspects he will still be learning them when they are old.

But they would agree on where the talking began. Not with the families, not with the photographs, not even with the engagement. It began on the thirty-first of August, at 5:19 in the evening, with a greeting.

Salaam.

It means peace. It is what you say to a neighbour, a stranger, a friend, or someone whose name you are still learning to hold carefully. A small word with a blessing folded inside it.

For a long time, Salaam was the beginning and end of many of their calls. Messages carried the rest. They did not rush what had not yet found its voice. Somehow, that was enough to begin.

# Part One · Salaam

*Late August to early October. A season of first words.*

## Episode 1 · The Afternoon of the Invitation

*Wednesday, 19 August, to Saturday, 22 August · Now playing: a piano, from another room*

Fate, Waqas would decide later, had a sense of timing no drama writer would dare to use.

It began as an ordinary afternoon. A family’s invitation travelled from one home to another, carried by phone calls, good intentions and the careful pauses of people choosing their words. Then the afternoon went quiet. An hour passed, then a few more. Somewhere in those hours, in a house he had never seen, a family weighed a question and came to an answer: yes. They would visit.

There was nothing dramatic about the answer when it arrived. It was simply the first opening in a story that neither family could yet see. Later, Waqas would remember the speed with which the visit was arranged, and how much had begun with that one yes.

Nobody told Waqas that his life had just turned a corner. Nobody ever does. He heard it as news about a name and a family, and he went on with his evening, and he did not yet know to be nervous.

The next day, Thursday, a little before Maghrib, an aunt walked into the room holding a phone. She had the composed expression of someone bringing news she already believed would matter.

“Look,” she said.

He looked.

A photograph. Light green clothes. A smile.

He had prepared a sensible response for this exact moment. It was the kind of thing a grown man says when he is being careful: Let me think about it. Let me see what everyone says. We’ll see. He had rehearsed it, on and off, for days.

What actually left his mouth was one word.

“Alhamdulillah.”

Not wow. Not she’s pretty. Thanks first. In his experience, that was how you knew a thing was serious: when the first feeling was not excitement but gratitude.

The aunt smiled the smile of a woman who has been proven right.

By eight that same evening, Shajer’s father and her uncle had come to meet him.

Two hours, from photograph to doorbell. The visit had happened quickly. Waqas was still trying to take in the photograph when the family arrived, and the evening seemed to have moved ahead of him.

Rare, Waqas thought, sitting up straighter than he had ever sat in his life. Fast. It feels as if fate is in a hurry.

Two days later, on Saturday at six, there was a second visit, and with it a second photograph. Dark green this time. There was a hint of nerves in the picture, but she was smiling.

She looked as beautiful as he had imagined. He had, after all, been imagining generously.

He still had not heard her voice. He did not have her number. He did not know whether she liked her tea sweet, or sang in the shower, or hated mornings.

None of that seemed to matter. A decision had begun to form inside him, quietly and without asking permission.

## Episode 2 · The Decision Before the Knowing

*Saturday, 29 August · Now playing: a guitar, unhurried*

They were engaged in the morning.

There were relatives, sweets, photographs and blessings, and the happy noise of a house full of people. On Shajer’s hand, Waqas would learn later, there was mehndi, dark and new, the kind of detail that makes a day feel permanent.

By late afternoon the house had emptied. A little before five, Waqas found a corner where the noise could not follow and sat down with the strange feeling of having been handed something precious before he knew how to hold it.

He thought about the girl in the photographs. He thought about how little he knew. He did not know her voice. He did not know her favourite song, or what she was afraid of, or how she took her tea. They had not exchanged a single word.

And yet he knew one thing, with a certainty so calm that it surprised him.

I am not letting go of her hand.

Not I might like her. Not let us see how it goes. Just that: a plain sentence, set down in a quiet chest, without any music.

Later, when he learned that her favourite film was Saiyara, he would smile at the strangeness of it. He had already decided to be the boy who stays. He had made that choice before he knew the story had a name for it.

In that corner, Waqas understood that his decision had arrived before his knowledge. He still knew almost nothing about her. He only knew that he wanted to keep choosing her. A feeling might change with the day; a choice had to be made again, and he intended to make it.

That night, a little after midnight, he found her name and pressed Follow.

He put the phone down, picked it up again, and checked whether she had accepted. He checked more than once through the night, until Fajr found him with the phone in one hand and a prayer mat in the other.

It was accepted the next day.

He was, he would later write, far too happy about it for a grown man.

## Episode 3 · The First Word

*Monday, 31 August · Now playing: a notification tone, slightly too loud*

The message took a long time to write, and it was meant to look as if it had taken none.

That was the whole art of it. It had to sound easy, a little playful, as if he were sending his first word to his new fiancée between two more important things. Waqas wrote it, deleted it, wrote it again, made it shorter, made it warmer, then changed it once more. He realised he was treating a greeting like a legal document.

At 5:19 in the evening, he pressed send.

> Assalamu alaikum.. 😊 i’m texting you assuming we know each other.. is that true..? 😇

Then came the three small words everyone learns to dread: Sent. Delivered. Seen.

The dots appeared. They vanished. They appeared again, and he held his breath long enough to feel it.

> Walaikum asalam absolutely.

Absolutely.

He read it once, then again, quite calmly. It was a single word, and it made his careful question feel unnecessary. Did they know each other? They had no conversations behind them, only two photographs and a great many opinions from aunts, and she had answered as if the answer were obvious. As if it had been waiting for someone to ask.

They exchanged three or four more messages. Small ones, polite, wonderful.

And then she was gone.

Waqas stared at the chat, waiting for it to resume. It did not.

He tried to be sensible. People had lives. People got busy. A person could not be expected to sit beside a phone because somebody else had finally found the courage to say hello. He told himself all this in a calm voice, and then he checked the phone again.

By nine o’clock he had produced three theories. She was busy. Her phone had died. She had read his messages, thought oh no, this is not what I imagined, and quietly closed the door.

The third theory was, naturally, the one he believed.

At her end, Waqas can only guess, and she is welcome to correct him. Perhaps the first exchange had been enough for one evening. Perhaps she needed a little room to breathe. He would later learn that opening up was difficult for her.

He could only imagine that she had been brave for those first messages and then needed to step back.

It was, he would come to think, entirely in character for the shyest person in the world. That was his affectionate description, not a claim that he could know what she had thought that evening.

He did not know any of that yet. That night he slept badly. In the morning, he tried to leave his phone alone for a while. It did not last.

The next day, a little after noon, the screen lit up.

> Assalamu alaikum, khairiyat?

Some messages look ordinary to the rest of the world and become landmarks to the person receiving them. This was one. She had come back. More than that, she had begun the conversation herself.

Waqas read it, smiled, and tried to look unmoved. It is difficult to look composed when you are alone in a room and very happy.

He answered in under four seconds, which he regretted at once.

That was how it began: with a vanishing and a return. The first Salaam was his. The next one, when it mattered, was hers.

He did not know it yet, but Assalamu alaikum would become the doorway of their whole story. The greeting would remain familiar even as more words gradually found their way between them.

## Episode 4 · Did You Eat?

*September · Now playing: soft guitar, a kettle humming in the background*

They did not leap into each other. That was the first thing that was right about them.

There were no long confessions, no speeches and no pretending to have known each other for years. There were small steps: a question, an answer, a pause, another question. They were learning the pace of one another without trying to hurry it.

On the fourth of September, they exchanged photographs.

It was a simple thing, and it felt enormous. Until then, each had been assembling the other from fragments: a name, a family story, a greeting, a few typed sentences and photographs passed between relatives. This one was different. It was sent on purpose, from her to him and from him to her. A face had finally been attached to the words. Waqas looked at it for a long time. For once, he did not try to think of something clever to say.

At first, messages did most of the work. There were voice notes and brief calls as the weeks went on, but conversation took longer to arrive. Neither of them seemed in a hurry to force it.

It was not a formal rule. It was simply the pace they found: careful, respectful, and their own.

Somewhere in those first weeks, Waqas began to ask a question.

The question became a familiar part of their exchange.

Have you eaten?

He asked it the way it had always been asked in his world: at the door, at the end of a call, by his Ammi, by the aunts, by everyone who loved someone and did not have the vocabulary for it. In the house where he grew up, have you eaten? was never only about food. It meant: Are you well? Have you looked after yourself? Did anyone remember to ask? Is there something I can do, even from here?

At first it was simple. Had she eaten? Was she okay? Had her day been tiring? But a question asked with care, and asked again, can become a ritual. And a ritual can become a kind of shelter.

As the weeks passed, Waqas began to understand that the question carried more than its literal meaning.

The dramas Shajer enjoyed had given him another way to recognise it: in those stories, asking whether someone had eaten could be a quiet way of saying that you cared. Waqas did not need to turn the question into a speech. He kept asking it.

The words were ordinary. The care inside them was not.

He began to notice how often he reached for the phone with that one question in mind.

> Khana khaya?

He kept asking, after that. The only difference was that he had begun to understand how much the question could hold.

## Episode 5 · The Promise

*Monday, 7 September, and Wednesday, 9 September · Now playing: a single violin, and then the sound of a held breath*

It was not an anniversary. It was not a festival, or a birthday, or any date that anyone would have thought to circle. It was a Monday, and at five in the afternoon a message arrived that was long enough for Waqas to know, before he read a word, that it had been written carefully.

He read it standing up. Then sitting down. Then, for no reason he could explain, standing up again.

> I promise to stay with you till the very end of my life.
> No matter the circumstances, every up and every down, we’ll face it together.
> I will never leave you alone in anything.
> We’ll be each other’s better half, and build a life that’s loving, peaceful, and full of respect. 💞

Some sentences change the temperature of a room. This was one.

He had spent days wondering, in the unspoken way of a careful man, whether she felt even a fraction of what he felt. Now it was there in her own words: not a hint, not a joke wrapped around a feeling, but a promise.

She had said it first. He would remember that for the rest of his life.

He did not answer at once. He wanted to get it right. He wrote an answer and deleted it. He wrote another and deleted that too. Dinner came and went. The house grew quiet.

And then, at midnight, as the date turned over from the seventh to the eighth, he sent his own.

> Shajer,
> I promise to try my best, every single day, to make you happy.
> I pray we stay together till the end, through every high and every low.
> Let’s tackle it all as one team.
> I could never picture anyone else beside me.
> You hold the most special place in my heart.

He chose midnight on purpose. A promise, he felt, should be the first thing of a new day, not the last thing of an old one.

If anyone had asked him later when it began, he might have given the date of the engagement, or the first message. But the seventh of September would always matter. That was the day two people who had barely spoken made, in plain words, the biggest promise of their lives, and meant it.

Their first real vow, he would call it. Before the first call. Before the first laugh. Before either had heard the other speak at length.

*Wednesday, 9 September · 8:03 PM*

Two days later, Waqas sent Shajer a link to the small world he had begun building for her: For Shajer. She received it at 8:03 in the evening.

Her reply was brief:

> mashallah🤌🏻.

He kept that moment beside the promise. The app would grow over time, but the first gift was the link itself, and the fact that she had opened the door he had made.

## Episode 6 · Munnar, Mehndi, and a Voice

*Sunday, 13 September, to Wednesday, 16 September · Now playing: wind through tea hills*

The hills of Munnar were beautiful: green beyond the eye’s ability to measure, clouds drifting as if they had nowhere to be, roads winding away into mist. Waqas had gone somewhere lovely and found, with some astonishment, that he was missing someone who was not there.

It surprised him. He had known Shajer for only a short while. How could an absence already have a shape?

But he did. He missed her messages and the small habits of her day arriving on his screen. He missed the person who had begun to occupy the spaces between one task and the next, so gently that he had not noticed until he was away.

The mist rolled over the valley. He stood in front of one of the most beautiful views he had seen and thought about his phone.

The next day, a photograph reached him: her hand, and on it the fading mehndi from their engagement.

It was temporary, but it brought the day back to him. The promise had been made. The colour would fade; what they had promised was meant to last.

On the fifteenth, he woke to a voice note.

Her first.

Assalamu alaikum wa rahmatullahi wa barakatuhu.

He listened to a greeting he had read a hundred times become, suddenly, a person. It had breath and softness. It belonged to the morning in a way a typed message could not.

She had used the long form, the full blessing: peace be upon you, and the mercy of Allah, and His blessings. It was the complete greeting, not the short Salaam he knew from their messages.

He played it again. And again. His own voice was still something he was working up the courage to send.

The next day they found another way to be together.

It began with stone-paper-scissors, argued over text with the gravity of an international tournament. Then, because that was clearly not enough, Ludo. Waqas won the first round. Shajer won the second, and celebrated with a run of lucky rolls and a single crown emoji.

He accepted his defeat gracefully for almost four seconds.

Neither of them could have said who was winning overall. It hardly mattered. They had found another way to spend time in each other’s company, and both were happy either way.

That, Waqas thought later, was a good sign. They could lose a game and keep smiling.

## Episode 7 · Without Any Expectation

*Monday, 21 September, to Thursday, 24 September · Now playing: a violin, one long note, held*

On the twenty-first, Shajer told him she missed him.

She told him she missed him.

He read the message more than once. It became one of those sentences he kept returning to, alongside the promise and the voice note, a small thing that had found a permanent place in his memory.

Two days later, at 3:07 on Wednesday afternoon, the twenty-third of September, Waqas did something that took courage.

He typed it.

> I LOVE YOU.. no matter what.. fovervee and ever and ever till infinity.. without any expectation till the last minute.. Inshallah.. that will be my dua..

He noticed the typo the next day. Fovervee. He decided not to apologise for it. It was, he felt, an honest word: forever typed too quickly by someone who meant it.

He had written without any expectation, and he meant it. That turned out to be the difficult part. He did not ask her to answer. He told himself he was not waiting by the phone.

That was not entirely true. He waited.

The next afternoon, at half past four, he did something braver than the confession. He sent his first voice note.

It was two words long: Assalamu alaikum.

He recorded the greeting more than once. The first attempts did not sound quite right to him. Eventually he sent the version he could live with, because a voice did not have to be perfect to be true.

Four days passed, counting from the confession. He did not ask for an answer.

Loving someone without expectation was a discipline. At first it felt like standing still in the rain. Then, slowly, it began to feel like trust and prayer.

## Episode 8 · Three Rings for Fajr

*Sunday, 27 September, and Monday, 28 September · Now playing: a phone, buzzing on a wooden table*

On Sunday, at 12:51 in the afternoon, his screen lit up.

> I LOVE YOU SOOOOOO DAMMNNN MUCH....... 🖤❤️💞ki mein apni jaan bhi lagadun aap pe.....

He read it standing in the middle of the room, and for a few seconds the room appeared to have been rearranged around him.

She had written it in capital letters, with all those extra O’s and N’s, the way a person writes when there is simply too much to fit into the available space. Then the second half softened into the language of the heart: ki mein apni jaan bhi lagadun aap pe. That she would give even her life for him.

For someone who found it difficult to open up, it was an enormous sentence. Waqas understood that much. He treated it carefully.

Four days apart, under the same sky. Two confessions. One promise taking shape between them.

He did not reply with something clever. He wrote that his heart had never been so full, then put the phone down gently.

The next morning, a little after five, his phone began to shake against the wood.

He woke the way you wake from a dream you did not quite want to leave: slowly, and then all at once. The screen glowed in the dark. Three missed calls. At 5:01.

The name on the screen was:

> ruhi.. ❤️

That was how he had saved her. Ruhi, from ruh, the soul. He had typed it in one evening, quietly, without telling anyone, because it seemed to him that she was the one person who had arrived in his life and made everything else feel like the outer layer of something deeper. In her phone, he was Jaana. In his, she was his soul.

She had never called him before. In all those weeks, in all that careful, deliberate quiet, she had not once rung his number.

And now, at one minute past five on a Monday morning, she had called three times. Not to ask for anything. Not to say anything. Only to make sure that he opened his eyes in time for Fajr.

Waqas sat on the edge of the bed and held the phone against his chest.

Out of every call a heart can receive, he thought, this was the one that mattered most. She had not called him for the world. She had called him for his prayers. A woman who wakes you for Fajr loves your soul more than she loves the world. In the dark, with the first grey of morning at the window, he felt that his heart had been placed in very good hands.

He stood, and made wudu, and went to pray. It was the best Fajr of his life.

At 1:11 that same afternoon, the day before their one-month mark, Waqas did something he had never done for anyone.

He sang.

It was his favourite song, Mera Mann Kehne Laga. He sang it low and a little unsure, in a voice that had not been trained or polished or rescued by any machine. There was no studio and no autotune. There was only a man, a phone and a quiet room.

He sent the fourth.

He did not know what she thought. It would be a long time before he stopped replaying the minutes that followed. But he knew what the song was for: the day before their month-mark, and the day after her confession. He had nothing to give her except something entirely his: his voice, and the courage to use it.

When words fall short, he told himself, melody remembers.

That became part of their morning rhythm. Three rings at five. A prayer. A day that began with her Good Morning and ended, very softly, with her Good Night. The rings grew. One day, he would wake at 5:11 to a lock screen stacked with nineteen missed calls. He would remember that screen for a long time.

Anyone else would see nineteen missed calls.

He would see how deeply she cared that he woke on time.

## Episode 9 · The Call He Didn’t Decline

*Wednesday, 30 September, and Wednesday, 7 October · Now playing: rain beginning, very softly, on a window*

At 11:01 that Wednesday night, with the house asleep and the world turned down to a hum, Waqas made a promise.

It was a different kind of promise from the first. The first had been about staying. This was about choosing, a choice he had made in a quiet corner on a Saturday in August and had been remaking every morning since.

> i Choose you.. over everything.. ONLY YOU Hameshakeliye.. if life give me 1000 more time to relive i will choose you and thats a promise and i pray for it.. Inshallah.. ❤️💞💝💗💓💕🖤💟

He sent it before he could edit it, which was how he knew that it was true.

Her answer came quickly, and it was quiet.

> so do i... inshallah... we will pray for the same ❤️💞

A thousand lifetimes. Out of every soul in every one of them, he would choose this one. And, as he understood from the three small dots and the dua folded into her reply, so would she.

On the seventh of October, at two minutes to eleven at night, his phone rang with something it had never rung with before.

This time it was a video call. The little screen turned bright. Waqas saw that she was calling and froze.

Waqas froze.

He understood in an instant what it was: her first video call. She had opened a new kind of door, one that brought her face and voice into the same moment. He understood what it would mean to answer.

Their calls had mostly been brief greetings. This was different. To answer would be to step into a conversation face to face, and he was not ready. But declining felt wrong, too.

So he did the only thing he could manage. He held the phone in both hands and let it ring.

He watched until the call ended. The screen went dark. A minute later, she wrote.

She wrote a minute later.

> Why didn’t you decline the call?

He answered honestly, in his own jumbled way.

> mannn nahi kar raha tha.. you called aur kaise mai decline karte bina wajah.. if i had a reason to decline makesense.. but bewajah nahi..

A pause. And then, because she could not help herself:

> so in future there might be a reason if u decline my call 😂

He laughed out loud, alone in the dark. It was a fair point. Life would have its reasons: work, unavoidable hours, days when the phone had to stay silent. He made himself a private promise that if he ever could not answer, he would explain why. He would not ignore her without reason.

It is odd the way a love grows. Not through grand gestures, but through a call that no one picked up and no one declined. Through a ring that was allowed, with great tenderness, to finish.

She had called. He had let it ring. Somewhere inside that unanswered call, Waqas understood how much courage it had taken her to make it.

# Part Two · The Distance Between

*October. When kilometres learned their names.*

## Episode 10 · 190 Kilometres

*Monday, 19 October · Now playing: an engine, a long road, a song turned very low*

Waqas had made the journey from Ambur to Chennai more times than he could count. For him it was familiar: a road, a few hours, a stretch of highway where the tea stalls came in the same order. He knew the route by heart.

That Monday evening he packed his bags with the slow movements of someone who knew the journey was ordinary and the leaving was not. He was going to Chennai to stay until the wedding. It was sensible. It was the right thing. In practical terms, it was simply a plan.

It did not feel like a plan.

At seven o’clock, he stood at the door of his home with the bags beside him. In another house in Ambur, Shajer was crying.

He knew because she had told him. Not in the careful shorthand of their usual messages, but plainly, as though the coming distance had made it impossible to be anything but plain.

> aaj pehli baar i feel chennai isn’t just 3 hours away... bohot door lag raha

He read it twice. For the first time since he had known her, he did not know what to say, and so he said what was true.

> ambur se chennai.. that’s usual for me. par kabhi mujhe aisa heavy feel na hua jaisa ab hai.. i don’t know how to express...

He could not say it better. It was perhaps the truest sentence he had typed, made almost entirely of the shapes of things he could not name.

He knew she felt the distance too. He did not need to guess at more than she had told him. They were in different houses, only a few streets apart, and already the road to Chennai seemed longer than it had on any map.

He drove.

A hundred and ninety kilometres. The highway unspooled in the dark with its tea stalls, lorries and long tired curves. Waqas had told her he would message the moment he reached, and he meant it. His phone sat on the dashboard mount, silent and lit.

He did not play music. He tried, and it felt like an intrusion.

Around midnight he reached Chennai, and there the evening, which had so far gone almost according to plan, quietly lost its mind. There was no parking. Then there was parking, but the wrong kind. Then a lane, a turn, a second lane, a man who waved him on and another who waved him back. He circled the same streets while the promised message remained unsent.

He knew she was waiting for the message. That was what made the delay difficult.

They were used to ending the day with a long conversation. Tonight there was only a screen and a clock. She had stayed awake because he had promised to tell her when he reached. He was still trying to find somewhere to stop.

When he finally parked, he sat in the car for a minute and breathed.

Then he typed a quiet Good night and told her to sleep. He did not want to keep her awake any longer. It was only two words, but he knew what they carried.

A message can cross 190 kilometres in a second.
A car cannot.

That night he understood the difference.

Chennai had always been three hours on a map: ordinary, routine, a place you drove to on a Monday. That evening, every one of those 190 kilometres felt different. Waqas understood that home had begun to mean more than a house with a gate.

Home was no longer a place.

Home was her.

## Episode 11 · Before We Sleep

*Wednesday, 21 October · Now playing: a clock ticking in a quiet room*

Two nights later, at eleven, Shajer asked him for a promise.

They were in the middle of the long conversation they shared before sleep, a habit that had grown, since the distance began, into a kind of shelter. Nothing particular had happened. There had been no argument, no misunderstanding, no wound. Then the screen lit up with a message that sounded as if she had been carrying it for a while.

> jaana kabhi agar we’d an argument toh please sone se pehle we will sort out haan

Then, in a second bubble:

> u can’t be angry wid me.. 😂🤭 and not talking to me

And then, quieter, with no emoji at all:

> i seriously can’t take that

Waqas read it slowly.

At the top of their chat, pinned where neither of them could ever miss it, was an older message of hers. The little banner showed only the beginning, cut short by the width of the screen: *u know right it’s difficult for me to open up... bu…* He knew the rest. It was the kind of sentence a person writes when she is trusting you with something she has never handed to anyone before. What came after the but was hers, and he kept it as carefully as he kept everything else she had given him.

He understood that this message had cost her something. For someone who found opening up difficult, it was not a casual request. It was a way of telling him what she needed him to protect.

He did not hesitate.

> pakka.. jaaan.. aur mai bhi..

Promise, jaan. And me too.

She added one more line, a little shyly, with a hand over her mouth:

> yeh toh ache se yaad mein rakho 🤭

Remember this properly.

> aapne bola aur kaise haam bhul sakte hain..

You said it. How could I forget?

Waqas understood that this was not a promise never to disagree. No two people who loved each other could manage that. It was a promise about what they would do when they disagreed: they would not let a quarrel become a night's silence. They would talk until they found their way back to each other, even if it took time.

In their story, ego would have to bow to love.

## Episode 13 · The Storm and the Seal

*Saturday, 31 October · Now playing: rain on a tin roof, then, slowly, nothing*

There is a kind of day that has no villain.

# Part Three · Beyond Salaam

## Episode 15 · Fourteen Seconds

*Sunday, 1 November · Now playing: a bird, the first of the morning, entirely out of tune*

She woke him for Fajr at 5:35, as she did each morning.

## Episode 16 · Wake Up, Jaana

*Thursday, 5 November · Now playing: nothing at all. Only a morning.*

There was one thing left. He had not known it was left until it arrived.

Their voices had begun to occupy more than a few seconds: a thirty-eight-minute conversation, a confession at dawn, and the short calls that still shaped their mornings. But the fifth brought something different. It was an ordinary sentence, spoken in the middle of an ordinary morning.

“Miss you.”

That was all.

Wake up, Jaana.

And he did.

## Episode 17 · The Boy Who Could Not Confess

*Thursday, 5 November, evening · Now playing: a laptop fan, a keyboard, and the occasional sigh*

That evening, Waqas thought about the heroes in the dramas Shajer loved.

They often stood in the rain, walked away when they should stay, or looked at the person they loved as if the truth were written on her face and impossible to say aloud. He used to find them ridiculous.

Now he understood something of their hesitation.

He had written promises, sent confessions and saved dates, screenshots and voice notes. Over many evenings he had built a small world around their memories, because he wanted Shajer to be able to return to them. Yet some feelings still went shy when he tried to say them aloud.

> I have something to ask you…

> 5 Nov. Morning. Wake Up Jaana.

There was a lot left to write.

## Episode 18 · Sixty-Six Minutes

*Friday, 6 November, 9:22 in the morning · Now playing: a long breath, and then, very faintly, a laugh*

He had rehearsed nothing.

That, afterwards, was what he found most remarkable. A man who had drafted a first message, recorded a two-word voice note and built a place for feelings he could not yet say aloud had arrived at the most important conversation of the autumn without a script. It was an ordinary Friday morning. The light was ordinary. At 9:22, the phone connected.

Who dialled first remained a friendly matter of dispute. He remembered it one way; Shajer might remember it another. Neither needed to win.

“Assalamu alaikum.”

“Walaikum assalam.”

Earlier in the autumn, a greeting often filled the whole call: Assalamu alaikum, Walaikum assalam, and then Allah hafiz. By the time of this morning’s call, they had already spoken at length once, but the familiar opening still carried a little of its old nervousness.

This time, nobody reached for the door.

The silence lasted a second, then two. Waqas could hear her breathing. The old panic stirred: say something, anything. Ask about the weather. Ask about the day.

Then, from the oldest corner of his heart, from his Ammi’s doorway and every aunt who had ever cared for someone through food, the question arrived on its own.

“Khana khaya?”

There was a pause, then a small laugh.

“Not yet,” said Shajer. “You?”

“Then why are we both…”

“I don’t know,” he said. “I wanted to ask.”

She laughed again. It began as a breath and grew into something he wanted to hear again. He knew he would spend a long time trying to be the cause of it.

They talked about ordinary things. Nothing was resolved; no one made a speech. The conversation moved from one subject to another, and the minutes gathered without either of them having to push them along.

They spoke about the morning, about food, about the distance between Ambur and Chennai, and about the small events that make a day. The subjects mattered less than the fact that the conversation had become easy enough to continue.

For him, that was the miracle.

The app held a record of the things they had saved: dates, messages, calls and promises. It was no longer only a page he had made. It had become a place where the story could be kept.

“It was designed to be.”

“I chased it for a very long time, Jaana.”

“That was the plan.”

“You’re terrible.”

“I know,” said Waqas, and could hear, in the way she said nothing at all, the smile on her face.

He asked what colour she might wear on the thirty-first. She kept it a secret. He said he would not tell anyone. She did not believe him. Then she gave him one small clue: he would recognise it.

He carried that clue through the rest of the day.

Somewhere in the second half-hour, the silences began to change.

At the beginning, silence had been a danger. Now it was simply a pause. They could stop talking without panicking, listen for a moment, and find the next thing to say. Waqas understood that silence had not always been an obstacle. Sometimes it had been the space in which they learned each other.

He looked at the call timer. He had been measuring these small calls without meaning to: six seconds, seven, ten, fourteen, and all the other fragments that had brought them here.

Sixty-six minutes.

Near the end, Shajer told him she had thought she would have nothing to say. Then she realised she had not run out.

“And?”

“And I don’t think I’ve run out.”

He closed his eyes.

By the time the call ended, Waqas understood what the title of this story meant to him: there was more than Salaam.

It was a small realisation, but it changed the shape of the morning.

They said Allah hafiz in the end, because the world, with its clocks and chores, insisted. This time it did not feel like a door closing. It felt like a promise to return.

Afterwards, he sat with the phone in his hands and did nothing for a while. Sixty-six minutes. He had lived a whole new kind of morning.

Then he opened the project, and under the card for the fifth of November he began to type.

> 6 Nov. Friday. 9:22 AM. The Conversation.
> Before their first long call, their voices had arrived in brief pieces: voice notes, missed rings and calls that lasted only seconds. On the first of November, one call lasted thirty-eight minutes. On this Friday morning, they spoke for sixty-six. She was the shyest person in the world, and yet time seemed to loosen around her voice, filling his morning with warmth and comfort.

He saved it. Below it, the last card was waiting, as it had been all week.

> Still Writing.
> Fifty-five days to go.

This is where the true pages of this story end, and where the promised ones begin.

# Part Four · The Fifty Days

*A hopeful fiction. This part has not happened yet. It is an imagined future, written with love rather than prophecy. The scenes, conversations and reactions here are wishes, not memories. They are invitations to a life the two of you have not yet lived.*

## Episode 19 · Day Fifty

*Wednesday, 11 November · Fifty days to go · Now playing: a message arriving, very quietly*

The file was ready at ten past nine in the evening.

## Interlude · Two Screens, One Thread

In the future Waqas imagined, they eventually chose a drama to watch together. It began, as so many of those stories did, with a character who would not say what he meant.

> WHY WOULD HE NOT JUST SAY IT

> Because then the drama would be over in two episodes.

> THAT IS NOT A REASON

> It is the ONLY reason.

He imagined that the best part would not be the episode itself, but having someone to share it with. Perhaps Shajer would disagree with the ending. Perhaps she would send a message after the credits. In this version of the future, there would be time for both.

A few evenings later, in another scene he hoped they might one day live, Shajer told him about the red thread.

> Have you heard of the red thread?

He had not. In the imagined conversation, she explained the old story: two people meant to find each other are joined by an invisible thread at the smallest finger. It can stretch, tangle and take years to draw them close, but it cannot break.

> So no matter what happens, they find each other in the end.

Waqas read it again, then answered with one word.

> Naseeb.

> Same story, he answered. Different language.

He liked the thought that two traditions could meet without either becoming smaller. Her thread and his naseeb could sit side by side: two ways of imagining a hope.

## Episode 20 · The Umbrella Episode

*Sunday, 6 December · Twenty-five days to go · Now playing: rain, and an orchestra that knows exactly when to enter*

The aunts held a vote about the biryani.

It had been scheduled for a Sunday afternoon at her house in Ambur, with both families present and a great many cups of tea. It concerned how much biryani should be prepared for a wedding in which, as everyone agreed, nothing could be allowed to go wrong. In Ambur, a wedding without biryani is merely a meeting. Opinions were offered. An uncle raised a point about quantity that had to be settled by a show of hands.

Waqas, who had driven the hundred and ninety kilometres that morning, sat straight on the sofa and tried to look as if he had strong views about biryani.

He did not have strong views about biryani. He had one view, and she was sitting across the room in light green.

She had not said a word about the colour. Neither had he. They were both being careful not to look at each other, and the aunts had noticed everything while continuing to discuss rice.

They had not been in the same room for weeks. Now they were separated by a carpet, three generations and a great deal of good behaviour.

Shajer stepped out onto the veranda to see a relative into a car and discovered she had no umbrella.

Waqas stood.

“Assalamu alaikum,” he said.

“Walaikum assalam,” she said.

“I have an umbrella.”

“I can see that.”

She stepped in.

They stood a decent distance apart, one an aunt could have measured and approved. Rain tapped hard on the umbrella. To hear each other, they had to lean a little closer.

He tilted the umbrella towards her.

He tilted the umbrella towards her. The cloth leaned her way, and his shoulder began to get wet.

“You’re getting wet,” said Shajer.

“It’s only rain.”

“The scene where the hero gets wet on purpose and the heroine says you’ll catch a cold, and then the music starts.”

“No music,” he said.

“This is the part,” said Waqas, “where I’m supposed to say something unforgettable.”

“I forgot it.”

“And then…” He thought. “She brings him soup.”

“Soup,” said Shajer, with deep satisfaction.

“Khana khaya?” said Waqas.

She turned to look at him properly, face to face, without a phone between them. For a moment he saw the smile she had so often hidden behind her hand.

“I wanted to see your face when you answered.”

“Yes,” said Shajer. “Biryani. Two plates. Don’t tell anyone.”

She stepped out from under the umbrella, back towards the doorway, and turned at the threshold with the light behind her.

“Twenty-five days, Jaana,” she said.

Then she went inside, and he stood alone on the veranda with one wet shoulder, a closed umbrella and the feeling that, somewhere, a director would have called the scene perfect.

## Episode 21 · Before We Sleep, Again

*Wednesday, 16 December · Fifteen days to go · Now playing: a clock, ticking louder than it should*

Wedding planning, said everyone who had survived it, is not a party. It is a military operation conducted with tea.

By the middle of December, the thirty-first had acquired a gravity of its own. Lists multiplied. Halls were confirmed and reconsidered. Relatives called with questions already answered, and others supplied answers to questions not yet asked. Waqas, in Chennai, handled part of it by phone between work and travel. Shajer, in Ambur, stood at the centre of the preparations, with her family around her and everyone holding an opinion about something.

At seven in the evening, he wrote: Assalamu alaikum.
Walaikum assalam.

“Walaikum assalam.”

Waqas stood very still.

He thought of the things he could say: that it would never be difficult, that he would never be tired, that every Tuesday would be golden. He knew none of those promises would be true.

A pause.

“Who writes them?”

“We do,” he said. “One card at a time. Remember the last one in the timeline? Still Writing. I didn’t put that there because I ran out of room. I put it there because it is the best part of the whole app.”

“I remember.”

“It still is,” she said. “But I’m trying. I don’t want to put everything away and pretend I’m fine.”

“No,” she admitted.

And he did. He stayed on the line while she went to the kitchen in a sleeping house. He heard a spoon, a plate, and the small ordinary sounds of someone looking after herself while he stayed with her from a hundred and ninety kilometres away.

The promise had held again. Later, if anyone asked, they would probably say the disagreement had been about wedding arrangements. It did not need a more dramatic name.

## Episode 22 · The Last Week

*Thursday, 24 to Wednesday, 30 December · Seven days to go · Now playing: a shehnai, far off, tuning up*

After that, the days went by like pages being turned very fast by a person who wants to see what is on the next one.

The house in Ambur filled with relatives. Suitcases arrived and were opened in rooms where they did not belong. A cousin lost something he had been asked to look after. The smell of cardamom and ghee drifted down the lane, and an uncle reported that the cauldrons had been ordered. In Chennai, Waqas packed again, this time to return to Ambur. The wedding was close enough now to make every small task feel final.

On the night of the twenty-eighth, a photograph reached him.

Two hands, held out against a dark cloth. Mehndi, deep and dark and new, climbing over the fingers in vines and flowers, and curling in small tight spirals over the wrists.

He looked at it for a long time.

The last time he had seen her mehndi, it had been the fading colour of the engagement, a reminder of a day already past. This was new, dark and still fresh. In the imagined days ahead, it felt like a promise written in henna.

> Find it, she wrote.

> Find what?

> You know what.

He did. It is an old wedding game: somewhere in the design, tucked into a curl of vine, the groom’s initial is hidden, and he must find it or lose a point to a roomful of cousins. He leaned close to the screen. A spiral, a leaf, a flower, a petal. In the crook of the left palm, almost invisible among the curls, was a small tilted W.

He found it quickly. He told her it had taken him twenty minutes.

> Liar, she wrote, and he could hear her smiling.

On the twenty-ninth, a small envelope reached him in Chennai by the hand of a cousin sworn to secrecy and not very good at it.

Inside was a single piece of red thread. Thin, plain, a little frayed at one end. It had no note, except for six words in her handwriting on the back of the envelope.

> Same story. Different language.

He held it for a minute. Then, with some difficulty, he tied it around his own wrist. It was almost nothing: a piece of thread. But in this imagined future it carried two languages at once, her red thread and the word his grandmothers had used for what was written.

Naseeb, he thought. Written, and walking toward me all along.

He decided he would wear it under the cuff of his sherwani. Nobody would see. He would know.

On the last night, he did not sleep. The house in Ambur had gone quiet. Waqas was staying a few streets away, close enough to feel the same town settling around him, far enough for the families to keep their wedding-eve customs.

It was just past one in the morning on the thirty-first. The house around him was quiet. The circle on the Big Day page had nearly closed, a thin line of light left open at the top, the width of a single day. He opened the Miss Counter, and looked at the button, and pressed it.

> I miss you right now.

In the very same second, his phone buzzed in his hand.

He looked down. On the screen, two hearts had begun to beat, side by side, in perfect time.

She had pressed it too.

He did not message. He did not need to. He sat on the edge of the bed in the dark with the phone in his hands, thinking of the small beat that, in the app, had answered his.

One day to go, he thought. And then, because it was already past midnight: No. Today.

## Episode 23 · Qubool Hai

*Thursday, 31 December · Zero days to go · Now playing: nothing yet. The music is waiting for the right second.*

At 5:01 in the morning, his phone rang once.

He had been awake since three. He had lain in the dark, and prayed, and stared at the ceiling, and tried to imagine the day, and failed, because it was too large to fit in his head. When the screen lit up with *ruhi.. ❤️*, he answered before it could ring a second time.

“Assalamu alaikum.”

“Walaikum assalam. Wake up, Jaana.”

“I’ve been awake since three.”

A pause. A small laugh, nervous and bright, with a whole sleepless night inside it.

“Me too,” said Shajer.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. It was the old silence, the one that had once frightened them both, and now it simply stood between them like a doorway with the light on.

“Fajr?” she said.

“Done.”

“Good.”

“Shajer.”

“Haan?”

He had a hundred things to say, and could not choose one. “See you at half past eleven,” he said.

“Allah hafiz, Jaana.”

“Allah hafiz.”

It was the last Fajr they would pray apart. For the first time since the beginning of the story, the two words at the end of the call meant exactly what they said: God keep you, until a few hours from now.

By seven, Ambur smelled of biryani.

It rolled out of the cauldrons that had been lit before dawn, thick and golden and impossible to ignore, drifting down the lanes and through the gates and into the folds of every sleeve. Somewhere a man was shouting a very important instruction about rice. Somewhere a child was running with a paper cup, being chased by an aunt. Uncles in good shirts walked up and down with the careful, dignified air of people who have been told their job is to look as if they are not worried.

Ammi found him in the corridor.

She stood in front of him, and without a word began to straighten his collar. It did not need straightening. She straightened it anyway, with the small firm movements of a woman who has been doing this since he was a boy, and then she looked at his face, and her eyes were wet.

“She asks you if you’ve eaten,” said Ammi. “Doesn’t she?”

“Every day.”

Ammi nodded slowly. “Then I’m not worried,” she said. “A girl who asks that every day, and means it, will always know how to look after you.”

Under his cuff, where nobody could see, a thin red thread pressed against his wrist.

The hall was full by eleven.

He sat where he had been placed, in the middle of a great many people, and found that he could see every detail and none of it at once. The aunt who had once held out the phone was in the second row, already crying into a handkerchief. Her father and her uncle were there, upright and quiet. A friend leaned over and whispered, “Breathe,” and Waqas realised that he had not been.

The Qazi arrived. A hush fell over the room, not suddenly but gently, as water settles.

The Qur’an has a name for what was about to happen: Mithaqan ghaliza. A solemn covenant. A weighty bond. He had read the words many times. Now, with the hall holding its breath, he felt their weight.

The Qazi, with two witnesses, went to ask her.

He waited. That was the whole of it. A man sat in a room, and in another room, a few walls away, someone he loved was being asked one question, and nothing in the world could hurry her answer.

11:24.

11:27.

He thought of the first photograph in light green. He thought of the first message and the word absolutely. He thought of the corner on engagement day and the plain decision he had made there: I am not letting go of her hand.

The boy who stays, he thought. It had become a promise he meant to keep.

The door opened. The Qazi came back with the witnesses. One of them met Waqas’s eyes across the room and gave, very slightly, a nod.

And then the question was put to him.

He heard it as if from a long way off: her name, the terms, the solemn plain words that have been said at the start of a hundred thousand marriages. His throat had closed. He opened his mouth.

“Qubool hai.”

It came out smaller than he wanted.

“Qubool hai.”

Steadier.

“Qubool hai.”

On the third, his voice broke, and it did not matter in the least.

It was eleven thirty. In his pocket, through the cloth, he felt his phone buzz once. He did not need to look. A circle on a lock screen, which had been closing since the twenty-ninth of August, had finally closed.

The hall breathed out.

“Alhamdulillah,” said somebody, and then everybody did, a great soft wave of it passing through the room. The dua began. Hands rose all around him, palms open. He raised his own, and found that they were shaking, and that he had never been so happy in his life. When it was over he was hugged by a great many people, in no particular order, including a stranger, and including, with unexpected violence, the uncle who had told the story four times.

Ammi did not say anything. She held his face in both her hands for a long moment. Then she kissed his forehead, and let him go.

They brought her to him a little later.

He did not see her come in. He saw the room change, the way a room changes when the light does. And he turned his head.

She was wearing green.

Not one green. Two. A deep, dark green, the green of the second photograph, the one with the nervous smile, and over it, falling softly from her head to her shoulders, a dupatta of the palest light green, the green of the very first picture he had ever seen of her, on a Thursday evening before Maghrib, in an aunt’s hand.

She had told him, on the phone, that he would recognise it.

He did.

She sat beside him, and for a moment neither could look at the other. In a hall full of people and raised phones, Shajer lifted a hand towards her face. Waqas recognised the gesture. It had been part of the story long before this day.

“Assalamu alaikum,” he said.

His voice was not quite his own.

She lowered her hand. She looked at him, properly, with her whole face, for the first time as his wife.

“Walaikum assalam,” said Shajer.

The same two words. The same greeting that had begun everything, at 5:19 on a Monday in August, in a chat window. It had once been almost all they could say to one another.

Now it was the first thing.

“You can say more than Salaam now,” she whispered.

He thought of the messages, the promises, the confessions, the songs, and all the mornings that had brought them here.

“Khana khaya?” said Waqas.

She laughed, a small, startled sound, and it undid something in his chest that he had been holding together for months.

“You can ask me that from right here now,” said Shajer.

“I have a lifetime of more,” he said.

“Show-off,” she said, and her eyes were bright.

Around them the hall went on, with its cameras, cousins and great noise of love. In the middle of it, two people who had once begun with a single greeting sat side by side, with a whole life ahead of them.

## Episode 24 · Midnight

*Thursday, 31 December, night · Now playing: the sound of a house, finally asleep*

There is a curious fact, one every family in Ambur could have predicted: at their own wedding, the bride and groom are often the only two people who do not get to eat.

They were photographed, blessed, embraced and asked to hold sweets for a great many cameras. Everyone said the biryani was the best they had ever tasted. And the two of them, who had asked each other khana khaya? every day for months, forgot to eat on the one day when it mattered most.

It was past ten by the time the house grew quiet. They were alone at last, in a room with the day folding itself away. Waqas sat on the edge of the bed. Shajer sat beside him, her dupatta around her shoulders and her hennaed hands in her lap.

“Khana khaya?” he said, because he could not help himself.

“No,” said Shajer.

“Neither did I.”

They looked at each other. And then both of them started to laugh, helplessly, in the way that you laugh when you are too tired and too happy to do anything else.

Ammi, naturally, had thought of it. Behind the door sat a steel tiffin wrapped in a newspaper and towel. Biryani, still warm. On the lid was a note in Ammi’s unmistakable handwriting: Eat.

There was one plate, and there were two spoons, and a long grave debate about who would get the last piece of chicken, which Shajer won.

It was, he decided, the best meal of his life.

After Isha, they prayed together. The room grew quiet afterwards, and the gratitude between them needed no speech.

When it was over, he took out his phone.

“What are you doing?” she said.

“There are two pages left.”

He opened the app and took her to the page called Nikkahnama, with its two circles and its line of Arabic.

> Place and hold your thumb on your designated scanner, together, to seal.

She looked at the screen, and then at him.

She put her thumb on the left circle. He put his on the right. The phone lit beneath their hands, and the page filled with gold.

> Sealed.

Neither of them said a word. They did not need to.

“And the other one,” said Shajer, softly.

He knew which she meant. The Lovenama page was still blank. He had written its instruction long ago: this page was for the moment of love, when neither person needed to guess. He had promised not to fill it before that moment was real.

He turned the phone towards her. The blank page remained between them, not as an omission but as a space they could leave for another day. They did not sign it.

Later, when the house had finally fallen quiet, they stood together by the window. The clock moved towards midnight. There was no scene to perform and no music to wait for.

“Assalamu alaikum,” said Shajer softly, into the first minute of the year.

“Walaikum assalam, ruhi,” said Waqas.

It was the same greeting that had begun their story. It had not changed. The life around it had.

# Epilogue · Still Writing

*Friday, 1 January 2027 · Fajr · Now playing: a piano, from another room, slowly, and then a little faster*

The phone did not ring.

It was the first morning in four months that it had not. No three rings at five. No ruhi.. ❤️ glowing in the dark. No lock screen stacked with missed calls, no sleepy Salaam, no half-minute of breathing across a hundred and ninety kilometres. The phone lay on the table, face down and quiet. Its work had changed.

Waqas woke anyway.

He woke in a room he was still learning. The light was grey. The year had turned in the night, and the house was quiet.

And then, close, in a voice that had no line in it, no static, no distance, only the plain warm air of a room, someone said:

“Wake up, Jaana.”

He turned his head.

Shajer was sitting at the edge of the bed with her hair loose and her dupatta around her shoulders, already wudu-fresh and bright-eyed. She looked at him with an expression he could not quite name.

Her voice sounded different without a phone between them. It was also the same.

“I was already awake,” said Waqas.

“Liar.”

“I was thinking about it.”

“About what?”

“About how nobody had to call.”

She laughed softly, and the grey room got warmer.

They prayed Fajr together. When it was over, and the light at the curtain had begun to change, he opened the project on his phone. There was one more thing to write.

He scrolled down to the end of the Timeline, where the last card had been waiting since November.

> Still Writing. More memories coming, starting with the thirty-first of December.

He smiled. He had never changed that card. It had never needed changing. But now he added two above it.

> 31 Dec. 11:30 AM. Qubool Hai.
> 1 Jan. Fajr. Nobody had to call.

And then, for the last time that morning, he went back to the final card, and with his thumb he changed one word.

> Still Writing. More memories coming, starting today.

The first Tuesday arrived on the fifth of January, as Tuesdays do.

It was an ordinary day. Waqas was late home. Shajer was tired. The tea had gone cold on the counter, and a small, unnecessary disagreement had grown out of a long day. Neither could afterwards remember exactly what had started it.

Then one of them looked at the clock.

“Sone se pehle,” said Shajer.

“Pakka,” said Waqas.

They sorted it out before sleep. It took a little time, one fresh cup of tea and the willingness to apologise before pride could get in the way.

Before he put the light out, he opened the project one last time. He added a card.

> 5 Jan. Tuesday. Nothing happened. Everything did.

He showed it to her.

She read it slowly, with her chin on his shoulder, with the lamp making a small gold circle on the page. She did not say anything for a long time.

“Nobody shows the Tuesdays,” she said at last.

“We do,” said Waqas.

On the screen, beneath the new card, a small line of text glowed in the dark:

> Still Writing.

# A Letter to Shajer

*11 November 2026*

My Ruhi,

If you are reading this, you have reached the end of the story I wanted to give you. It is not the end of our story. It is only where these pages stop.

I am not always good at saying large things aloud. You know that. You have heard my voice hesitate on Assalamu alaikum, and you have never made me feel foolish for it. So I did what I often do when my voice runs out. I wrote.

I wrote down the photograph in light green, the first Salaam, the day I chose you, your promise and mine, the calls for Fajr, the messages, the distance, and the morning when a familiar greeting became an ordinary sentence spoken in your voice. I wrote the moments I could remember and tried not to pretend I knew the ones I could not.

Then I wrote a future. The umbrella, the wedding, the quiet after the guests, the first Tuesday. Those pages are not memories. They are hopes. I wrote them because I want a life in which we keep finding one another in ordinary days, not only in the beautiful ones.

I do not know every detail of what is to come. I hope there will be rain, tea, laughter, and arguments we learn to settle before sleep. I hope we keep asking each other whether we have eaten. I hope we keep making room for the truth, even when it is difficult to say.

I know one thing. If life gave me a thousand more times to live it, I would choose you. I said it once. I will go on saying it.

Fifty days, Ruhi. I am counting. And I hope the days after them will give us more stories than these pages can hold.

Yours, from the first Salaam and every morning after,
Waqas
